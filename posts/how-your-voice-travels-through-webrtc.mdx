You say, “Can I move my appointment to Friday?”

Your browser sends the sound in small pieces. A receiver turns those pieces back into audio, and the voice agent decides when it has enough information to answer. The reply makes the return trip to your speaker. WebRTC carries the media; the conversation system decides what to do with it.

A connected call can still get that sentence wrong. “Friday” can reach recognition damaged or late. A pause before it can trigger an early answer. And when you correct yourself with “Actually, Monday,” the old reply can keep playing.

We'll follow those moments through one call. If you're already investigating a failure, jump to [the symptom table](#pipeline-diagnostics).

## Capture and Encoding

The microphone's signal becomes samples: numbers representing sound over time. The browser exposes an audio track through `getUserMedia()`. Before encoding it, capture processing can suppress noise, adjust gain, and cancel sound returning from the speaker. The [Media Capture specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/mediacapture-streams/) defines the constraints and settings exposed to applications.

A recording taken before that processing can differ from what the browser sends. If “Friday” sounds clear locally but recognition gets it wrong, the recording point matters. “Local audio” is too vague to establish that the network changed it.

<span id="a-frame-isnt-a-word" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="audio-frames" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="smaller-packets-have-a-cost-too" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="packet-duration" aria-hidden="true" />

The encoder works on stretches of sound, without waiting for word boundaries. For this walkthrough, assume Opus with **20 ms of audio per packet**: 50 packets per second of continuous transmission. A packet can cut through a vowel or contain the end of “to” and the beginning of “Friday.” Losing 20 ms of silence and losing 20 ms of that date have different consequences for the request.

Twenty milliseconds is an example, not a WebRTC constant. Opus supports different frame lengths, and packets can contain multiple frames. Collecting more audio per packet adds waiting; shorter packets increase header and packet-processing overhead. Neither choice removes delays elsewhere in the call. [RFC 7587](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7587.html) describes the Opus packet format; [RFC 6716](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6716.html) defines codec features including silence suppression and error correction. Support doesn't establish that a feature is active in a particular session.

## Connection Setup

The audio needs a working connection before the sentence can travel. Loading a website establishes access to its web server, but the media connection has its own setup. The application exchanges session information, the endpoints test possible paths, and they establish media security. That work doesn't repeat for every sentence.

In this call, the browser's remote endpoint is the media server. The agent has another media connection, and a remote speech recogniser can be further away still. A browser's connection statistics describe its leg of that journey.

<span id="ice-candidates" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="sometimes-the-working-route-needs-a-relay" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="turn-relays" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="connected-doesnt-yet-mean-readable-audio" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="transport-security" aria-hidden="true" />

<details>
<summary>The connection protocols, if you need them</summary>

**Signalling** carries setup messages through an application-chosen channel, such as a WebSocket. **SDP** describes the session, including codecs and connection information. An offer sent over a WebSocket doesn't mean microphone audio follows it. See the [WebRTC API](https://www.w3.org/TR/webrtc/) and [ICE offer/answer specification](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8839.html).

**ICE** tests candidate pairs and selects a working path. Local addresses, public mappings discovered with **STUN**, and relay addresses provide possible candidates. It doesn't search the internet for the globally fastest route. [RFC 8445](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8445.html).

A **TURN** relay forwards traffic for as long as its route is in use. Its location and network conditions affect the journey. TURN over TCP or TLS can help on networks that block UDP, although ordered TCP delivery can make later data wait behind earlier lost data. See [RFC 8656](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8656.html) and [WebRTC transport requirements](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8835.html).

**DTLS-SRTP** establishes keys for **SRTP**, which protects the media. Voice packets don't travel inside a WebRTC data channel. Encryption to a media server doesn't by itself hide audio from that server; end-to-end encryption is a separate design. An agent must have access to intelligible audio at an authorised endpoint. See the [WebRTC security architecture](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8827.html) and [LiveKit encryption overview](https://docs.livekit.io/transport/encryption/).

</details>

## Jitter Buffering

The packets carrying “appointment to Friday” don't necessarily arrive at even intervals. Their RTP sequence numbers help identify order, while RTP timestamps position audio on the sender's media timeline. A timestamp isn't a packet's arrival time. [RTP specification](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3550.html).

<figure class="article-illustration">
  <Image src={jitterImage} alt="Unevenly spaced audio fragments enter a receiver, which sends a continuous waveform to a speaker." widths={[480, 800, 1200, 1672]} sizes="(min-width: 1280px) 784px, (min-width: 1024px) 55vw, (min-width: 832px) 792px, calc(100vw - 40px)" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Holding a little audio back helps smooth uneven arrivals.</figcaption>
</figure>

For Opus over RTP, that clock runs at **48,000 ticks per second**. Our 20 ms packets advance by 960 ticks, regardless of the sample rate later required by speech recognition. [Opus timestamp rules](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7587.html#section-4.1).

This synthetic sequence separates the two clocks:

| Packet | RTP timestamp | Arrival at this receiver |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4100 | 96000 | 0 ms |
| 4101 | 96960 | 23 ms |
| 4102 | 97920 | 61 ms |
| 4103 | 98880 | 62 ms |

The media timestamps advance evenly. The final two packets arrive just a millisecond apart. Playing audio according to arrival time would carry that irregularity into the sound.

<span id="smooth-audio-means-waiting-somewhere" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="jitter-buffers" aria-hidden="true" />

A **jitter buffer** gives the receiver time to absorb uneven arrivals. The lab below shows the tradeoff: more waiting can save a packet from missing its playback deadline, but delays the stream. Switch between steady and uneven arrivals, then change the playback start. These are illustrative schedules, not measurements from your browser.

<JitterLab client:visible />

A real receiver does more than hold packets. WebRTC's NetEq adapts its buffering, speeds or slows audio, conceals missing stretches, and joins recovered audio back into the stream. Continuous sound can therefore include replacement audio. At the agent, that repaired sound becomes material for recognition. [NetEq implementation guide](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/refs/heads/main/modules/audio_coding/neteq/g3doc/index.md).

<span id="a-late-packet-may-already-be-useless" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="late-packets" aria-hidden="true" />

Once playback has passed a missing stretch, a late packet can't change what was heard. Recovery has to meet a useful deadline. A burst of missing packets also removes a longer continuous stretch than the same losses scattered through a sentence, so a loss percentage alone can't establish whether “Friday” survived. [WebRTC media transport](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8834.html).

## PCM Batching

The agent receives decoded samples, then prepares them for speech-to-text. That preparation can change sample rate, channel count, or chunk size. Labelling 48 kHz samples as `16000` doesn't resample them: it tells the receiver to interpret the same samples against a different clock. Sample count divided by samples per second must describe the intended duration.

Even correctly formatted audio can wait in an application queue. The following **synthetic scheduling example** isolates that wait. It uses no microphone, WebRTC connection, or recognition service, and is not a browser benchmark.

Suppose five decoded chunks, each representing 20 ms of audio, become ready at 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ms. One policy forwards each chunk immediately. Another holds all five until the last is ready. Both receive exactly the same input schedule.

Run this JavaScript in Node.js or a browser console:

```js
const readyMs = [20, 40, 60, 80, 100];
const batchReadyMs = readyMs[readyMs.length - 1];

const rows = readyMs.map((ready, index) => ({
  chunk: index + 1,
  readyMs: ready,
  immediateMs: ready,
  batchedMs: batchReadyMs,
  addedWaitMs: batchReadyMs - ready,
}));

console.table(rows);
```

All times below are milliseconds. Immediate forwarding matches the ready time.

| Chunk | Ready | Batch sent | Added wait |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 20 | 100 | 80 |
| 2 | 40 | 100 | 60 |
| 3 | 60 | 100 | 40 |
| 4 | 80 | 100 | 20 |
| 5 | 100 | 100 | 0 |

The first chunk waits **80 ms longer before being forwarded toward STT**. Transport is unchanged in this model; the delay is introduced after chunks are ready. A network trace could look identical under both policies.

The last chunk adds no wait because it completes the batch. This is a bounded collection delay. A queue that grows throughout the call points to a different problem: consumption is falling behind production. Giving that queue more capacity lets it retain older audio; it doesn't make the consumer faster.

This result doesn't promise an 80 ms improvement in the caller's response time. It omits scheduling overhead, recogniser buffering, and downstream work. A recogniser might require larger chunks. The useful finding is narrower: observing chunk readiness and forwarding separately exposes waiting that a single “audio received” timestamp hides.

The [phone-line experiment](/blog/building-a-phone-line-in-pytorch) covers a different channel. Its telephony sample rates and codec assumptions shouldn't be transferred to this browser Opus stream.

## Turn Detection

“Can I move my appointment to… Friday?”

During that pause, a speech detector sees silence, but your request is unfinished. **Voice activity detection (VAD)** estimates whether speech is present. Turn detection decides whether the conversational turn is complete, using signals such as silence, recogniser events, or language context. WebRTC doesn't send a message meaning “this person has finished explaining.” [LiveKit turn handling](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/).

<span id="a-final-transcript-still-needs-a-definition" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="transcript-finality" aria-hidden="true" />

A recogniser's “final” result can finish a segment while the person intends to continue. Treating every final segment as permission to answer can cut off the date. Waiting longer helps preserve the continuation, but also delays replies to complete requests. The event's provider-specific meaning belongs in that decision.

<span id="starting-early-makes-cancellation-part-of-the-design" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="preemptive-generation" aria-hidden="true" />

An agent can prepare a reply before the turn is confirmed. LiveKit's [preemptive-generation settings](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/tuning/) allow that work to start early and be cancelled when the input changes. For our sentence, an early draft could be obsolete as soon as “Friday” arrives. Discarding a draft is straightforward; changing an appointment early creates an action that might need reversing. Speculation needs a boundary between preparing an answer and committing an action.

## Response Latency

The first generated text token isn't sound. Speech synthesis needs usable text, the application must publish its audio, and the returning media passes through the server and browser receiver before reaching the speaker. “Audio sent” might mean queued by the application, accepted by a library, or transmitted. Those events have different timestamps.

For the caller's initial wait, use:

```text
first audible agent speech − end of the caller's speech
```

<span id="the-quiet-part-of-the-chart-can-hide-the-problem" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="overlapping-work" aria-hidden="true" />

<ResponseTimeline />

Recognition can run while you speak. Reply generation can begin before the turn closes, and playback can begin before the whole answer exists. Adding every stage's duration as if they ran sequentially would double-count overlapping work. The relevant chain is the unfinished work holding back the first audible reply.

Agent metrics can help locate that chain. [LiveKit's observability data](https://docs.livekit.io/testing/observability/data/) separates turn, model, and speech measurements, but their boundaries aren't the speaker's acoustic output. A lab measurement of audible delay needs input and speaker output on a shared timebase. A software playback estimate should be labelled as an estimate.

Across machines, timestamps need documented clock alignment and uncertainty. Two processes reporting milliseconds since startup have unrelated zeros. If alignment uncertainty exceeds the apparent improvement, the trace doesn't establish that improvement. And first speech is only one boundary: a quick opening word followed by a long silence still makes a poor reply.

## Interruption Handling

The agent starts, “I can move your appointment to Friday at…” You interrupt: “Actually, Monday.”

<figure class="article-illustration">
  <Image src={interruptionImage} alt="A woman raises her hand while speaking to her laptop; her dark voice wave passes above a fading pale reply." widths={[480, 800, 1200, 1672]} sizes="(min-width: 1280px) 784px, (min-width: 1024px) 55vw, (min-width: 832px) 792px, calc(100vw - 40px)" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Stopping the reply means clearing queued audio as well as cancelling generation.</figcaption>
</figure>

Your correction travels inward while old reply audio is still travelling outward. Recognising the interruption doesn't empty those paths. The application needs to stop generation, clear pending speech, and reject chunks that arrive after cancellation. A response ID lets downstream consumers distinguish cancelled output from the new answer.

Cancelling one asynchronous request doesn't prove that every task it started has stopped. Nor can cancellation recall sound already played. The user-facing measure is how long the old answer remains audible after the correction begins.

<span id="the-next-answer-needs-the-conversation-you-actually-had" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="conversation-history" aria-hidden="true" />

Conversation history has a similar boundary. If the generated sentence was “Friday at three is available” but the caller heard only “Friday,” storing the whole sentence as delivered makes the next response assume information the caller never received. LiveKit documents [truncating interrupted history to the played portion](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/#interruptions). That depends on the system's playback information, so the saved conversation is part of an interruption test.

Speaker output can also return through the microphone and resemble new speech. Comparing headphones with the device speaker helps separate a real correction from a false interruption caused by the agent's own audio.

<span id="a-short-glossary-if-you-need-it" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="technical-glossary" aria-hidden="true" />

<details>
<summary>A short glossary</summary>

- **Sample rate:** samples representing one second of audio, per channel.
- **Codec / Opus:** a codec encodes and decodes audio; Opus is the codec assumed here.
- **Frame / packet:** a frame represents encoded audio over time; a network packet carries data and headers. They aren't necessarily one-to-one.
- **RTP timestamp:** position on a media clock, rather than a wall-clock arrival time.
- **Jitter / concealment:** variation in arrival timing; replacement audio used to cover a missing stretch.
- **SFU:** selective forwarding unit, a media server that forwards participant streams.
- **STT / LLM / TTS:** speech-to-text, large language model, and text-to-speech.
- **Playout:** delivering received audio for playback; a software event needn't coincide with sound leaving the speaker.

</details>

## Pipeline Diagnostics

A trace needs a direction and an observation point. Browser inbound-audio statistics concern the reply arriving at the browser, not microphone audio reaching the agent. Collect agent-side evidence for the incoming sentence.

For the browser leg, `RTCPeerConnection.getStats()` can expose concealment and jitter-buffer counters. To calculate average buffer residence over an interval, divide the change in `jitterBufferDelay` by the change in `jitterBufferEmittedCount`. That's an interval average, not an instantaneous peak. Compare the same stream; skip resets and intervals with no emitted samples, and account for browser support. A candidate pair's round-trip time also doesn't establish one-way delay when halved. [WebRTC statistics specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/webrtc-stats/).

| What you observe | First useful evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Slow join, normal later turns | Signalling, candidate checks, security setup, agent readiness |
| Broken audio during speech | Receiver arrival timing, loss and concealment; processing load |
| “Friday” becomes another word | Audio immediately before recognition, then the recogniser output |
| A reply starts before “Friday” | Speech activity, transcript events, and the turn-complete decision |
| Generated speech is heard late | Queue readiness and forwarding timestamps, return path, playback |
| Friday speech continues after “Monday” | Cancellation propagation, pending audio, late response chunks |

For the next test, replay one fixed “Can I move my appointment to… Friday?” recording through your integration. Capture audio after browser processing and immediately before recognition, and timestamp chunk readiness, forwarding, turn completion, and first playback. Keep the session ID, observation points, browser and library versions, selected route, and codec with the run. Change one condition, such as application batching, and repeat. The first boundary where the recordings or timings diverge gives you a specific component to investigate.
